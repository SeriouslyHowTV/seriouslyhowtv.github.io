How do you restart a dead power grid?

At 12:33 on Monday 28 April 2025, the power systems of mainland Spain and Portugal collapsed. Over 31 gigawatts of demand disappeared. The hard part came next, because most large power stations need electricity just to start.

Short answer: operators start a few special "black-start" plants that can run without outside power, usually hydro. Those plants energise pre-planned routes of lines, called cranking paths, to give startup power to bigger plants. Customers are then reconnected in small blocks so the frequency doesn't collapse again, and the small grids that grow, called islands, are synchronised and joined. Iberia did this in about 18 hours.

What happened on 28 April 2025

According to ENTSO-E, the association of Europe's transmission operators, mainland Spain lost 25,638 MW of demand and Portugal 5,900 MW: 100% of the load in both systems. The Balearic and Canary Islands, which have separate grids, were not affected. Traffic lights went dark and trams and trains stopped where they stood. ENTSO-E's expert panel later called it the most severe incident in the European power system for more than 20 years.

Why a dead grid can't simply be switched back on

A large thermal power station is full of machines that need electricity before the plant can make any. Pumps move cooling water. Fans push air through the boilers. And the generator needs an excitation current to magnetise its spinning rotor. Engineers call these the plant's auxiliaries, and in normal operation they are fed from the same grid the plant supplies.

When the whole grid is down, that loop is broken. Many plants have backup diesels for exactly this reason, but backups can fail. When South Australia's entire system went dark on 28 September 2016, the Australian Energy Market Operator found that one gas plant's backup diesel tripped after about five seconds, and the plant could not start.

Black-start plants

Every grid therefore keeps a few plants that can start from nothing. Hydro is the classic choice: open the gates, falling water spins the turbine, and only a small local supply is needed for controls. Spain's restoration plan, as described by ENTSO-E, divides the country into seven areas, each with at least two hydro plants able to black start. Portugal's plan relies on one gas plant and one hydro plant.

Cranking paths: power stations first, homes later

The first job of a black-start plant is not your house. It is other power stations. Operators energise a pre-planned route of lines and substations, called a cranking path, to deliver startup power to larger plants. In North America the reliability standard EOP-005 requires restoration plans to identify these paths in advance, so nobody is improvising in a control room with the lights out.

Bringing customers back, block by block

A small, newly restarted grid is fragile. Every block of customers reconnected slows the generators a little, and the frequency dips. Connect too much at once and the frequency can fall far enough for protection to trip the units again. The US grid operator PJM gives a rule of thumb for the largest single load step: about 5% of a unit's rating for a steam unit, about 15% for hydro and about 25% for a gas turbine. After a long outage, customers also tend to draw more than usual when reconnected, because appliances all restart at once, which is why this stage is slow and deliberate.

Islands and synchronising

Restoration produces islands: separate small grids, each running at its own frequency. Two islands can only be joined when their frequency, voltage and phase angle match. Operators watch a synchroscope and close the breaker as the two waveforms line up. Closing it out of step forces the machines into alignment violently and can damage the equipment the restoration is trying to save.

How Iberia came back

Neighbouring grids helped. Ten minutes after the collapse, the first line from France was energised, and half an hour after it Morocco was supplying power through the subsea interconnector. Not every black start worked; some hydro units tripped. But islands grew steadily.

What caused it

There was no single cause and no cyberattack. The ENTSO-E expert panel's final report describes many interacting factors. In the half hour before the collapse, the grid experienced oscillations. Then voltage in the south of Spain rose, generators disconnected to protect themselves from the overvoltage, and each disconnection pushed the voltage higher still, until the system could no longer hold together.

Not every grid comes back in a day

Eighteen hours is fast. In the 14 August 2003 blackout, about 50 million people in the United States and Canada lost power, and 61,800 MW of load was interrupted; some areas waited up to four days. Restoration also depends on the health of the plants. Cuba, where many oil-fired stations are more than 40 years old and fuel is scarce, had suffered its sixth nationwide grid collapse of 2026 by early August, according to UPI.

Why restoration is rehearsed

Speed like Iberia's comes from planning and practice. In North America, every black-start unit must be tested at least once every three calendar years. Great Britain's Electricity System Restoration Standard requires 60% of regional demand to be restored within 24 hours and all of it within five days, from the end of 2026. The grid is not a switch. It is a machine that has to be woken up, one plant, one line and one neighbourhood at a time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a black start?

A black start is restarting a power system after a total or partial shutdown without help from the external grid. It begins with special power stations, often hydro plants, that can start using only their own resources.

Why can't a power station just restart itself?

Large thermal plants need electricity before they can generate any: pumps for cooling water, fans for the boilers, and the excitation current that magnetises the generator's rotor. These auxiliaries are normally fed from the grid.

How long did it take to restore power in Spain after the April 2025 blackout?

About 18 hours. The collapse happened at 12:33 on Monday 28 April 2025, and 99.95% of Spain's demand had been restored by 07:00 the next morning. Portugal's transmission network was restored by 00:22.

Was the Iberian blackout caused by a cyberattack?

No. The Spanish government's committee found no evidence of a cyberattack, and the ENTSO-E expert panel described many interacting factors, including voltage control problems, oscillations and a cascade of generators disconnecting under overvoltage.

Why do engineers restart the grid in islands?

Small separate grids are easier to keep stable. Each island balances its own generation and load, and islands are joined only when their frequency, voltage and phase angle match, because connecting them out of step can damage equipment.
