Why did the Tonga tsunami arrive early? The meteotsunami, explained

After the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption on 15 January 2022, tide gauges across the Pacific started moving before a normal tsunami could have reached them. In Japan, sea level began to change about three hours before the expected tsunami. The reason was in the air.

Short answer: the eruption sent a pressure wave through the atmosphere, a Lamb wave, that travelled at about 310 to 320 metres per second and circled Earth. That is faster than a tsunami moves through the deep ocean. As the air wave crossed the sea it pushed on the surface and drove a sea-level wave of its own, a meteotsunami, ahead of the ordinary tsunami. It was recorded as far away as the Caribbean and the Mediterranean.

Two waves, two speeds

An ordinary tsunami travels through the water, and its speed depends on how deep the water is. In ocean about 4 km deep it moves at roughly 700 km/h, which follows from the standard shallow-water formula.

The Hunga Tonga eruption also launched a wave in the air. It travelled at about 310 to 320 metres per second (Matoza et al., Science, 2022; Amores et al., 2022; Imamura et al., 2022), faster than the tsunami in the water below it. So the eruption sent out two signals at once, and the one in the air got everywhere first.

What is a Lamb wave?

A Lamb wave is the kind of atmospheric pressure wave the eruption produced: a single pulse that spread outward from the explosion and travelled around the whole planet. Matoza et al. (Science, 2022) traced it circling Earth four times in one direction and three times in the other, over about six days.

Ordinary airport weather stations recorded it. In the public one-minute pressure records, we found the jump in Honolulu about 4.5 hours after the eruption, in Anchorage after about 8.5 hours, in Miami after about 10 hours and in Boston after 10 to 11.5 hours. More on the wave itself is in our article on the pressure wave that circled Earth four times.

What is a meteotsunami?

A meteotsunami is a sea-level wave driven by the atmosphere instead of by the seafloor. After the Hunga Tonga eruption, the driver was the Lamb wave. As it crossed the ocean it pressed on the sea surface, and the water responded with a wave that moved along with the air wave. That is why sea levels started to change before the ordinary tsunami could possibly have arrived.

The evidence on tide gauges

  • Hilo, Hawaii: in the NOAA tide-gauge record, the water level changes when the air wave passes, and the larger tsunami waves come later.
  • Japan: sea level began to change about three hours before the expected tsunami. At Amami, waves reached about 1.2 m (Imamura et al., Pure and Applied Geophysics, 2022; Japan Meteorological Agency).
  • Far from the Pacific: because the air wave circled the whole planet, the meteotsunami was recorded even in the Caribbean and the Mediterranean (Carvajal et al., Geophysical Research Letters, 2022; Devlin et al., 2022).

The ordinary tsunami still came

The early, air-driven wave did not replace the ordinary tsunami. The eruption killed at least three people in Tonga. In Peru, on the other side of the Pacific, two people drowned, and waves caused an oil spill at the La Pampilla refinery near Lima, initially estimated at about 6,000 barrels.

The volcano itself may also have made the tsunami larger. Surveys showed that the caldera floor dropped by about 700 m during the eruption, from about 150 m to about 850 m below sea level, displacing roughly 8.9 km³. A study published in 2026 suggests this rapid collapse may have enlarged the tsunami (Ribó et al., Nature Geoscience, 2026).

What we leave out

You may read that the tsunami near the volcano was bigger than Krakatau's or reached 40 m. That claim appears in press releases, but we could not verify it in the paper abstract, so we do not use it.
