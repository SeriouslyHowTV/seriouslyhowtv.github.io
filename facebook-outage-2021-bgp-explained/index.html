How one command took Facebook offline for six hours

On 4 October 2021, Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp didn't crash. They disappeared from the internet's map. Here is the sequence, based on Meta's own engineering post-mortem.

The setup

Facebook runs a private backbone network linking its data centres around the world. Its public-facing DNS servers (the ones that tell the internet where facebook.com is) were designed with a safety rule: if a DNS site can't reach the data centres, it should stop advertising itself on the internet, so traffic goes to healthier sites instead. That's normally sensible. On 4 October it became the trap.

Step 1: a routine command cuts the backbone

During routine maintenance, an engineer ran a command intended to assess the availability of global backbone capacity. It unintentionally took down all the connections in the backbone, disconnecting Facebook's data centres from each other and from the internet. A tool meant to audit such commands and stop dangerous ones didn't catch it.

Step 2: DNS servers withdraw their routes

The DNS servers saw that they could no longer reach the data centres and, following their safety rule, withdrew their BGP route announcements. (BGP is how networks tell each other what they can reach; see how the internet works.) The rest of the internet now had no path to Facebook's name servers, so names like facebook.com, instagram.com and whatsapp.com stopped resolving. Cloudflare watched Facebook's routes vanish at about 15:40 UTC.

Step 3: locked out of their own house

Engineers couldn't reach their systems remotely, because the tools they would normally use ran on the network that had just disappeared. Meta sent engineers onsite to the data centres, which are deliberately hard to get into and to modify, slowing the fix.

Step 4: switching it back on, carefully

Once the backbone was restored, routes were re-announced at about 21:00 UTC, and services came back over the following minutes. Meta's engineers were cautious: individual data centres had dropped their power use by tens of megawatts, and in their words, "suddenly reversing such a dip in power consumption could put everything from electrical systems to caches at risk." They brought traffic back in stages.

What it teaches

  • A single network can vanish from the internet without any cable being cut: the map is made of announcements.
  • Recovery tools need a way in that doesn't depend on the system they're meant to fix.
  • Restarting can be as risky as failing; large systems come back in stages.

Nine months later, a routing change inside Canada's Rogers took out service for more than 12 million customers for most of a day. What if every network did this at once? That's the thought experiment in our documentary: what would happen if the internet went down for 24 hours.

Frequently asked questions

Why did Facebook go down in October 2021?

During routine maintenance, a command meant to assess backbone capacity unintentionally disconnected Facebook's data centres. Its DNS servers then withdrew their BGP route announcements, so the rest of the internet could no longer find Facebook, Instagram or WhatsApp.

How long was the 2021 Facebook outage?

Nearly six hours. Cloudflare observed Facebook's routes disappearing at about 15:40 UTC on 4 October 2021, with service returning after the routes were re-announced at about 21:00 UTC.

Why couldn't engineers fix it remotely?

The tools they would normally use ran on the same network that had disappeared, so engineers had to be sent into the data centres, which are designed to be hard to get into.

Was it a cyberattack?

No. Meta's post-mortem describes a configuration error during maintenance, not malicious activity.
