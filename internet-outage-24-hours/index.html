What would happen if the internet went down for 24 hours?

The lights are on. Your phone has full signal. But nothing loads, anywhere. Here is what would break, what would quietly survive, and how close real outages have already come.

Short answer: apps, cloud services, online payments, airline check-in and much of office work would stop within the first hour. Phone calls and texts, GPS, the power grid, broadcast radio and TV, and emergency calls would mostly keep working, because they run on separate networks. A true global outage is extremely unlikely, but smaller real-world outages show exactly how it would feel.

First: what "the internet" actually is

The internet isn't one network. It's about 78,000 of them: phone companies, universities, cloud providers, small local ISPs. Each is an autonomous system with its own number. To reach each other, they constantly announce which addresses they can deliver to, using the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP). Put together, those announcements form the internet's map: more than a million routes, updated constantly.

On top of that map sits the Domain Name System (DNS), the internet's phone book, anchored by 13 root server identities run by 12 organisations and copied to more than 2,000 sites. Underneath it all, about 600 undersea cables carry more than 99% of international data. For the full picture, see how the internet actually works.

Our thought experiment: at midnight, every network withdraws its routes at the same moment. No cables are cut and no power is lost. The map simply goes blank, and even cached DNS answers stop helping, because they point to places no one can reach any more.

It has already happened, on a smaller scale

In October 2021, a faulty command during routine maintenance took down Facebook's backbone. Its DNS servers withdrew their routes, and Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp disappeared for nearly six hours. Engineers couldn't reach their own systems remotely and had to be sent into the data centres. (The full story, step by step.)

In July 2022, a routing change inside Rogers, one of Canada's largest carriers, knocked out service for more than 12 million customers for most of a day. Many people couldn't reach 911, and Interac debit payments failed across the country.

Hour 1: everything that lives "somewhere else" stops

Six billion users hit refresh. Cloud documents won't open, video calls drop, messaging apps freeze, ride-hailing apps can't find a car. Smart locks, cameras and thermostats lose the features that live in the cloud. Offices are open and computers are on, but staff who sign in through online identity services and keep files in the cloud can do very little.

Strangely, your phone can probably still call and text: most voice and SMS traffic crosses carriers' own networks, not the public internet. Wi-Fi calling and app-based messaging go silent, though, and as millions switch to ordinary calls, those networks come under heavy load.

At the checkout, many countries' card terminals are set to require online approval for every payment, so tap-to-pay fails. Cash is king again.

Hour 6: flights and shipping

Planes keep flying safely, because air traffic control runs on its own dedicated networks. But check-in, crew scheduling and bookings don't. We know how that looks: in July 2024, a faulty software update crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers, and airlines cancelled more than 5,000 flights in a single day.

Shipping suffers too. In 2017 the NotPetya cyberattack knocked out the systems of Maersk, one of the world's largest container lines. Port terminals slowed or stopped, staff worked by hand, and the company rebuilt about 45,000 PCs and 4,000 servers in around ten days, at a cost of up to 300 million dollars. Just-in-time supply chains have little slack: even one day leaves a backlog that takes far longer than a day to clear.

Hour 12: what keeps working

Calls and texts, mostly. GPS, because satellites don't need the internet (your phone may take a few minutes to get a first fix without data, and only downloaded maps will work). The power grid, whose control systems sit behind their own security perimeters. Emergency calls, which travel over phone networks and dedicated emergency networks. Hospitals switch to rehearsed downtime procedures: paper forms, printed lists, phone calls. And broadcast radio and TV become the way the world finds out what is going on. Hour 18: the cost

The world economy produces roughly 320 billion dollars of output per day. How much of that would stop is unknowable: a global outage has never happened. The closest measured cases are government-ordered shutdowns, which cost an estimated 19.7 billion dollars across 28 countries in 2025. There is a human cost too: without social media, news sites and messaging apps, information slows to the speed of a phone call, and rumours can travel faster than facts.

Hour 24: switching it back on

Routing changes can spread around the world within minutes, so the map redraws itself almost at once. But billions of devices reconnecting together flood servers, so engineers bring systems back in stages. When Facebook came back in 2021, its data centres had dropped tens of megawatts of power use, and Meta warned that reversing that dip too suddenly could itself put systems at risk.

Could it really happen?

Not all at once. There is no single off switch: tens of thousands of independent networks, many paths between them, and engineers watching for exactly this. Real failures stay local. That's what makes the thought experiment useful: it shows what we depend on, and what's worth keeping at home anyway, like some cash, a downloaded map and a battery radio.

Frequently asked questions

Could the whole internet really go down at once?

A true global outage is extremely unlikely. The internet is tens of thousands of independent networks with many paths between them, so real failures stay local, like Facebook in 2021 or Rogers in Canada in 2022.

Would my phone still work if the internet went down?

Probably for calls and texts. Most voice and SMS traffic crosses mobile carriers' own networks rather than the public internet. Wi-Fi calling and messaging apps would stop, and carrier networks could get congested.

Would card payments work without the internet?

Often not. In many countries card terminals are configured to get online approval for every payment, so tap-to-pay fails and cash becomes essential.

Does GPS need the internet?

No. GPS receivers get their position from satellites. Without mobile data the first fix can take a few minutes, and online maps won't load, but downloaded offline maps keep working.

How much would a one-day internet outage cost?

Nobody knows. World output is roughly 320 billion dollars per day. The closest measured case is government-ordered shutdowns, which cost an estimated 19.7 billion dollars across 28 countries in 2025.
