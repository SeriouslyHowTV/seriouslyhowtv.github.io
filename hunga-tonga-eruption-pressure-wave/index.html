The Hunga Tonga eruption: the pressure wave that circled Earth four times

At about 04:15 UTC on 15 January 2022, an underwater volcano in Tonga exploded with such force that the air itself rang. The pressure wave went around the planet four times in one direction and three times in the other, and instruments built for completely different jobs recorded it.

Short answer: the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption launched a Lamb wave, a pressure pulse that hugs the ground and travels at about the speed of sound. It circled Earth 4 + 3 times over roughly six days. Barometers, tide gauges and the nuclear-test monitoring network all recorded it, the wave dragged an early tsunami across the oceans, and an underwater avalanche from the eruption cut Tonga's only international internet cable for 38 days.

The volcano

Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai lies about 65 km north of Nukuʻalofa, Tonga's capital. Its two small islands were joined by an eruption in 2014–15. Activity resumed in December 2021, a large eruption followed on 14 January, and the climactic explosion came the next day, at about 17:14 Tonga time.

The blast in numbers

  • Plume height: about 57 km, the highest volcanic plume ever recorded and the first known to reach the mesosphere (Proud, Prata and Schmauß, Science, 2022). Pinatubo's 1991 plume reached about 40 km.
  • Umbrella cloud: in GOES-17 satellite images we measured it at about 150 km across a quarter of an hour after the onset and about 500 km within an hour.
  • Lightning: the most intense ever recorded, peaking at 2,615 flashes per minute, about 192,000 flashes in total (Van Eaton et al., GRL, 2023).

A wave that went around the planet

The explosion pushed the atmosphere outward as a single pressure pulse. This is a Lamb wave: it travels along the surface at about 310 to 320 metres per second and loses little energy, so it can circle the whole Earth. Matoza et al. (Science, 2022) traced four passages in one direction and three in the other, over about six days.

You do not need special equipment to see it. Ordinary airport weather stations log air pressure every minute. In the public ASOS records, the jump arrives in Honolulu about 4.5 hours after the eruption, Anchorage after about 8.5 hours, Miami after about 10 hours and Boston after 10 to 11.5 hours, and it comes back again roughly a day later from the other side of the planet.

Every instrument on Earth heard it

The International Monitoring System, built to detect secret nuclear tests, has 53 infrasound stations. All 53 detected the eruption, the largest event the network has ever recorded, above the 2013 Chelyabinsk meteor (Vergoz et al., EPSL, 2022). In the film you can hear the Hawaii station's record sped up 600 times. People in Alaska, about 10,000 km away, reported hearing booms. A cloud camera at the Gemini North observatory on Maunakea filmed the wave crossing the sky, and NASA's ICON satellite measured winds up to about 720 km/h in the upper atmosphere.

The tsunami that arrived too early

As the air wave crossed the oceans it pressed on the sea surface and pulled a small sea-level wave along with it. This is why tide gauges started moving before a normal tsunami could have arrived. In Hilo, Hawaii, the water level changes when the air wave passes, and the larger tsunami waves come later. In Japan, sea level began to change about three hours before the expected tsunami. The air-driven wave, a meteotsunami, was even recorded in the Caribbean and the Mediterranean, seas with no direct path from Tonga.

The eruption killed at least three people in Tonga. In Peru, on the other side of the Pacific, two people drowned and waves caused an oil spill at the La Pampilla refinery near Lima.

How the eruption cut Tonga off the internet

Tonga had one international submarine cable, linking it to Fiji. The eruption set off an underwater avalanche of sediment, a turbidity current, that ran for more than 100 km. Because the cable broke at known places at known times, scientists could measure its speed: up to 122 km/h, the fastest underwater flow ever measured (Clare et al., Science, 2023). Tonga was without a full internet connection for 38 days, until the repair ship Reliance replaced 92 km of the 827 km Tonga–Fiji cable and service returned on 22 February 2022.

What the seafloor revealed

Surveys after the eruption showed that the caldera floor dropped by about 700 m, from about 150 m to about 850 m below sea level, displacing roughly 8.9 km³. A study published in 2026 suggests that this rapid collapse may have made the tsunami larger (Ribó et al., Nature Geoscience). The eruption also put about 146 million tonnes of water into the stratosphere, around 10% of what was already there, and it stayed for years (Millán et al., GRL, 2022).

What we deliberately leave out

You will often see the explosion described in megatons. Published estimates of its "yield" differ by a factor of ten, so we do not quote one. Its net effect on climate is also still debated: early studies suggested slight warming from the water vapour, and later work points to a small net cooling from sulfate.

Frequently asked questions

How many times did the Hunga Tonga pressure wave go around the Earth?

Four times in one direction and three times in the other, over about six days, according to Matoza et al. (Science, 2022). Barometers around the world recorded each passage.

How fast did the Hunga Tonga pressure wave travel?

About 310 to 320 metres per second, close to the speed of sound in air. This kind of wave, which hugs the surface and travels around the whole planet, is called a Lamb wave.

Why did the Tonga tsunami arrive early?

The air pressure wave pushed on the ocean as it passed and dragged a small sea-level wave along with it, faster than a normal tsunami travels. In Japan, sea level began to change about three hours before the expected tsunami arrival.

How long was Tonga without internet after the eruption?

38 days. An underwater avalanche from the eruption broke Tonga's only international cable, and full service returned on 22 February 2022 after the repair ship Reliance replaced 92 km of the 827 km Tonga–Fiji cable.

How high did the Hunga Tonga plume go?

About 57 kilometres, the highest volcanic plume ever recorded and the first known to reach the mesosphere (Proud et al., Science, 2022). Pinatubo's 1991 plume reached about 40 km.
