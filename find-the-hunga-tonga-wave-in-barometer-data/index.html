Find the Hunga Tonga pressure wave in your nearest airport's barometer data

On 15 January 2022 the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption sent a pressure wave around the whole planet. Airport weather stations wrote it down, minute by minute, and those records are free to download. In this activity students predict when the wave should reach a station near them, then go and find it.

Short answer: download one-minute station pressure for 15–21 January 2022 from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (IEM) ASOS one-minute page, predict the arrival time as eruption time + distance ÷ 312 m/s, and plot pressure against time around that moment. You will see a sharp jump and dip. The wave came back from the other side of the planet roughly a day later, and it kept circling for about six days.

What you need

  • A computer with internet access and a spreadsheet program.
  • The eruption time: about 04:14–04:15 UTC on 15 January 2022 (17:14 Tonga time). We use 04:15 UTC.
  • The volcano's position: about 20.55°S, 175.39°W.
  • The wave speed: this kind of wave, a Lamb wave, travelled at about 310 to 320 metres per second. We use 312 m/s.

Step 1: Pick an airport station near you

The data come from ASOS, the automated weather stations at US airports. The IEM one-minute archive is built from the one-minute data that NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) publish for ASOS sites in the United States, so pick a US station. Alaska and Hawaii are included: Anchorage (PANC) and Honolulu (PHNL) are two of the stations we used. Large airports such as Miami (MIA), Boston (BOS) and Denver (DEN) are a good place to start.

Outside the United States? The same IEM page points to a separate download of routine hourly observations, which covers sites over the entire globe. With one reading an hour you will see much less of the wave, so for a first try use a US station even if it is far away. Distance makes the prediction step more interesting anyway.

Step 2: Download one-minute pressure for 15–21 January 2022

Open the IEM page ASOS One Minute Data Download (mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/request/asos/1min.phtml). The form has eight numbered parts. Choose:

  1. Select Station: your station (you can select more than one).
  2. Timezone of Observations: UTC Time. Everything in this activity is in UTC, which avoids time-zone and date mix-ups.
  3. Select Start/End Time: start 2022, Jan, 15, 12 AM, minute 0; end 2022, Jan, 21, 11 PM, minute 59.
  4. Select Variables: Sensor 1 Station Pressure [inches]. (Sensor 2 and Sensor 3 also exist; use one of them if Sensor 1 has gaps.)
  5. Data Sampling? Every Minute. The page notes that the coarser options pick out readings rather than averaging them, so they can skip right over a short jump.
  6. How to view? Download to Disk.
  7. Data Delimitation: Comma, which gives a CSV file a spreadsheet can open.
  8. Include Station Latitude & Longitude values? Yes. You will need them in Step 3.

Then press Process Data Request. The file has the columns station, station_name, lat, lon, valid(UTC) and pres1: about 10,000 rows per station for the week. Pressure is in inches of mercury. Missing readings appear as M, and a few minutes may be absent entirely; just skip them.

Shortcut for teachers: the IEM documents the same request as a web address (see the page's Backend Documentation). This link downloads the week for Honolulu; replace PHNL with another station code:

https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/cgi-bin/request/asos1min.py?station=PHNL&vars=pres1&sts=2022-01-15T00:00Z&ets=2022-01-22T00:00Z&sample=1min&what=download&tz=UTC&delim=comma&gis=yes

Step 3: Predict when the wave arrived

The wave spread out from the volcano along the Earth's surface, so the time it needs is the shortest distance over the globe (the great-circle distance) divided by its speed:

arrival time (UTC) = 04:15 + distance ÷ speed
with the distance in metres and the speed 312 m/s, the answer is in seconds; divide by 3,600 for hours.

To get the distance in kilometres in a spreadsheet, use the latitude and longitude from the downloaded file (columns C and D, row 2; south and west are negative) with the spherical law of cosines with an Earth radius of 6,371 km:

=6371*ACOS(SIN(RADIANS(-20.55))*SIN(RADIANS(C2))+COS(RADIANS(-20.55))*COS(RADIANS(C2))*COS(RADIANS(D2-(-175.39))))

Worked example, Honolulu. The file gives PHNL at 21.3187°N, 157.9224°W. The formula gives about 5,030 km. 5,030,000 m ÷ 312 m/s ≈ 16,100 s ≈ 4.48 h, or 4 hours 29 minutes. Predicted arrival: 04:15 + 4:29 = about 08:44 UTC. In our own analysis of the Honolulu record, the jump arrives about 4.5 hours after the eruption. The prediction works.

Look for the later passages too. The wave went around the planet four times in one direction and three times in the other, over about six days. The part that set off the "wrong" way reached your station from the opposite side, after travelling the rest of the way around the globe. With an Earth circumference of 2π × 6,371 ≈ 40,030 km:

  • first arrival, the short way: distance ÷ speed
  • the wave from the other side: (40,030 km − distance) ÷ speed. For Boston that is about 24 hours after the eruption, for Honolulu about 31 hours. This is the return "roughly a day later".
  • each further lap: add 40,030 km ÷ 312 m/s ≈ 35.6 hours

The real speed was not exactly 312 m/s everywhere, so later predictions drift. Search a window of a few hours around each one. In our Denver record for 15–21 January, the passages show up at the predicted times. The IEM's own Daily Features from 16 to 19 January 2022 animate four of these crossings over the United States. The second one came the other way round the globe and so reached the East Coast first.

Step 4: Plot it

  1. Open the CSV in your spreadsheet. Filter out the rows with M in the pressure column.
  2. Make a line chart with valid(UTC) on the horizontal axis and pres1 on the vertical axis. A whole week mostly shows the weather: slow rises and falls lasting hours to days.
  3. Zoom in: chart only the rows from about one hour before to one hour after your predicted time. That is a ~2-hour window, about 120 rows. Look for a sharp jump and dip that stands out from the slow drift.
  4. Or remove the weather: in a new column, subtract a running average. If pressure is in column F starting at row 2, put =F62-AVERAGE(F2:F122) in row 62 and fill it down. This takes each reading minus the average of the two hours around it. Chart that column: slow weather changes flatten out and the wave stands out.
  5. Optional: convert to hectopascals (hPa, the same as millibars) by multiplying inches of mercury by 33.8639.

For our film we used a slightly more careful filter, an 8–120 minute band-pass, which keeps only changes that last between about 8 minutes and 2 hours. The running average above is the spreadsheet-friendly version of the same idea.

Step 5: Questions for students

  1. How good was your prediction? Read off the time the jump starts. How many minutes early or late was it compared with your prediction?
    Teacher note: for our four stations the prediction fell inside the measured range or within about ten minutes of it. Picking "the start" of the jump is itself a judgement, which is why our own Boston and Anchorage values are given as ranges.
  2. Measure the speed yourself. Use speed = distance ÷ (observed arrival − 04:15 UTC). What do you get in m/s? Compare with 310–320 m/s.
    Teacher note: pooling results from several stations makes a good class graph: distance on one axis, arrival time on the other. The slope is the speed.
  3. Which station heard it first, and why? Put your class's stations in order of arrival.
    Teacher note: the order follows the great-circle distance from Tonga, not east–west position on the map. Honolulu, the closest of our stations, comes first; Boston, the farthest, comes last.
  4. Find the wave that came the long way round. Predict it with (40,030 km − distance) ÷ 312 m/s, then look for it. Does it look the same as the first arrival?
    Teacher note: the IEM author described the second passage of the eastbound wave as "not as strong as the first", and also noted differences in how strong the wave looked across the country that he could not yet explain. There is no single right answer here. Let students describe what they see.
  5. Why is Denver's number so much lower? Download Denver and Miami together and compare the pressure values.
    Teacher note: the variable is station pressure, measured at the station's own height, and Denver is high up. The wave shows as a change on top of whatever the normal value is, which is why subtracting a running average helps.
  6. Could something else make a jump like this? Think of weather events that change pressure quickly. How could you prove your jump is the volcano?
    Teacher note: whatever students suggest, the giveaway for the eruption is timing: the jump arrives when the distance-based prediction says it should, and it moves across every station in order of distance from Tonga.

Want more? The same pressure wave pushed on the oceans and dragged a small sea-level wave along with it: see why the Tonga tsunami arrived early. The full story of the eruption is in the pressure wave that circled Earth four times.

Frequently asked questions

Can I see the Hunga Tonga pressure wave in weather station data?

Yes. US airport weather stations (ASOS) record air pressure every minute, and the Iowa Environmental Mesonet offers that archive as a free download. In our own measurements the jump reaches Honolulu about 4.5 hours after the eruption, Anchorage after about 8.5 hours, Miami after about 10 hours and Boston after 10 to 11.5 hours.

How do I predict when the Tonga pressure wave reached my town?

Measure the great-circle distance from the volcano (about 20.55°S, 175.39°W) to your station and divide by the wave speed, about 310 to 320 metres per second (we use 312 m/s). Add the result to the eruption time, about 04:15 UTC on 15 January 2022.

Did the Tonga wave pass more than once?

Yes. It went around the planet four times in one direction and three times in the other over about six days (Matoza et al., Science, 2022). In a week of barometer data you can look for the wave that came the long way round, roughly a day after the eruption, and for later laps.

Is one-minute pressure data available outside the United States?

The Iowa Environmental Mesonet's one-minute archive comes from ASOS sites in the United States. The same site has a separate download for routine hourly observations from sites over the entire globe, but one reading an hour shows far less of the wave's shape.
